## 3
*Vallorna — Friday 7 October*
<!-- zb: chiusura=X -->

On Friday the wind swung round to the south and carried the smell of seaweed up to the square. Nives spent the morning at the hospital on the coast. Basilio was awake, with a bandage on his forehead and a drip in his left arm. He asked her about the lantern before he even asked who she was.

“It's been dark for three nights,” said Nives.

“Three nights.” The keeper closed his eyes. “Then someone has noticed.”

“The whole village has noticed.”

“No. Someone.”

He would not add anything else. When the nurse came in, he turned his head to the wall. Nives saw that he was still holding his right hand clenched, even in his sleep.

At sunset Nives went back to the pier. The tin door was open. Inside, Elda Corte stood in front of the electrical panel, with the long key in her hand. She did not turn round when she heard the footsteps.

“You found the key,” said Nives.

“I never lost it.”

Elda climbed the iron steps up to the padlock at the top. Nives followed her, one step below. From up there you could see the boats laid up and the nets spread out to dry. The chain knocked against the iron as it had that first morning.

“My father is sixty-three,” said Elda. “He comes up here four times a night. In March he slipped twice. In August three times.”

“You counted them.”

“I count everything. It's my job.”

“And the night of the third of October?”

Elda opened the padlock. Her hands were not shaking. She said she had pulled the switch down at midnight, so that her father would find the lantern dark and understand he was too old for that work. She said it looking at the sea, in the same voice as the shifts and the laid-up boats. She had not thought of the dark on the ladder. She had not thought he would go out anyway, without light, to check.

“Then you corrected the time in the register,” said Nives.

“Eleven forty was when I saw him go out. Eleven sharp was the right time for the shift.”

Nives wrote nothing. The notebook stayed shut in the pocket of her coat.

Elda went down to the electrical panel and pushed the switch back up. For a moment nothing happened. Then the lamp came on with an insect's buzz, and the light began to turn over the sea. It passed over the boats, over the pier, over Elda's face, and went back to the water.

Nives watched the light make its first full turn. She thought of the keeper's clenched hand and understood what he was holding inside it: nothing, only the decision to keep quiet. She went down the steps alone. At the end of the pier, the fisherman was waiting with his hands in his pockets.

“So was it the wind?” he asked.

Nives opened her notebook, slipped off the elastic and decided what to write.
