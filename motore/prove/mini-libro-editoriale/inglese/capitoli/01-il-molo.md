## 1
*Vallorna — Tuesday 4 October*
<!-- zb: chiusura=D -->

The fisherman called her at six, when the sea was still grey. Nives reached the pier with her blue coat buttoned up to her chin. The lantern at the end of the stone was dark. It had not happened in eleven years, the fisherman told her, and he said it twice.

Basilio Corte lay on the rocks below the iron ladder. He was breathing slowly, one hand open on the shallow water. The village doctor held his head still between his knees. Someone had brought a boat blanket, stiff with salt.

“He fell in the dark,” said the fisherman. “In last night's wind.”

“The wind rose at three,” said Nives. “The pier had been dark since midnight.”

The fisherman looked at his shoes. He had his hands in his pockets and did not take them out.

Nives climbed the steps of the lantern. The tin door was shut, but not locked. Inside it smelled of diesel and wet wool. The electrical panel had a single switch, as big as a thumb. It was down.

She looked at it for a long time without touching it. The wind does not pull a switch down. A hand does, and calmly too.

On the hook beside the door the long key was missing, the one for the padlock at the top. The lantern chain knocked against the iron with every wave. Nives counted the knocks up to ten, then went down.

“Who has the keys?” she asked the fisherman.

“Basilio. And his daughter, for the shifts. Nobody else.”

“Was the daughter here last night?”

“Elda sleeps in the village. She has the harbour registers to keep.”

The doctor raised his head and said the keeper had to go to the hospital on the coast. Two men lifted him in the blanket. Basilio opened his eyes for a moment, looked at the lantern and closed them again. He said nothing. Nives saw him tighten his right hand, as if there were still something inside it.

When the ambulance left, the pier was empty again. What remained was the blanket on the rocks and a dark stain on the stone. Nives opened her notebook and slipped off the elastic. She wrote the time of the call, the time of the wind, the switch pulled down. Then she wrote a single word and underlined it: key.

The fisherman had moved off as far as the nets. From there he watched her, and pretended not to.

Nives went back to the tin door and shut it. The sun was coming out of the sea behind her. It was the first day, in eleven years, that the village had woken without the light that turns. Who had turned the key, and why had they waited for the dark?
