## 2
*Vallorna — Wednesday 5 October*
<!-- zb: chiusura=R -->

The harbour office was above the fish market, in two rooms with a low ceiling. The gas stove whistled as soon as it was lit. Elda Corte turned it off when Nives came in, then turned it on again, as if she did not know what to do with her hands.

On the table there were three registers with cloth covers. One for the boats, one for the diesel, one for the lantern. Elda had already opened them at the right page.

“My father signed the shift at eight in the evening,” she said. “Lit at twelve past eight. Check at eleven.”

“And after eleven?”

“After eleven there's nothing. The next check is at five.”

Nives bent over the page. The keeper's handwriting was wide, with letters leaning to the right. The times were written in pen, all in the same black ink. All but one.

Next to the eleven o'clock check, someone had corrected the time in pencil. Before, it said eleven forty. Over it, in light graphite, it said eleven sharp. The mark was so fine you had to tilt the sheet against the window to see it.

“Did he write this?” asked Nives.

“I keep the registers. My father writes the times, I copy them out in the morning. That's two hundred and forty shifts since the start of the year.”

“I didn't ask you how many there are.”

Elda closed the diesel register. She put it on top of the other two, with the corners lined up. Outside someone was unloading crates of fish and the room filled with broken ice and voices.

“The fisherman says it was the wind,” said Elda.

“The fisherman says a lot of things. Where were you at midnight?”

“At home. Copying out the boat register. There are thirty-one boats, six are laid up with engine trouble.”

Nives noted the numbers without comment. Elda said them the way others say a prayer, one after another, so as not to say anything else. Her father was in a bed on the coast with his head bandaged. The daughter talked about stalled engines.

Before leaving, Nives asked for a copy of the page. Elda opened the desk drawer to take out a sheet. Inside were two black pens, an elastic band, an eraser. And a pencil sharpened not long ago, with a short, shiny point.

Elda pushed the drawer shut with her hip, without hurrying. Nives said nothing. She went down the fish market stairs holding the sheet folded in four. On the pier, at the far end, the lantern was still dark, and nobody had yet thought about who would light it again.

That pencil had written eleven sharp, and Nives now knew for whom.
